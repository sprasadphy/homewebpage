1. The Big Picture: Recreating the Primordial Universe

What is the universe made of? How did it come into existence? How does matter behave under the most extreme conditions imaginable? These fundamental questions drive the journey of high-energy nuclear physics.

In our everyday world, the matter around us is composed of atoms, whose nuclei contain protons and neutrons (hadrons). In turn, hadrons are formed of elementary constituents known as quarks, bound together by the exchange of gauge bosons called gluons. The governing theory of this strong nuclear force is Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD).

Unlike electromagnetic photons, gluons carry the "color charge" of the strong interaction and can interact among themselves. This self-interaction causes a phenomenon called color confinement: under normal conditions, quarks and gluons cannot exist as free particles. However, QCD also possesses asymptotic freedom: at extraordinarily high temperatures or energy densities, the interaction strength weakens, causing hadrons to melt into a deconfined, thermalised state of matter: the Quark-Gluon Plasma (QGP).

💡 Key Concept for Interdisciplinary Readers: What is QGP?

The Quark-Gluon Plasma existed naturally in the first few microseconds ($\sim 10^{-6}\text{ s}$) after the Big Bang before cooling into ordinary protons and neutrons. By accelerating heavy atomic nuclei (such as Lead-208, $^{208}\text{Pb}$) to $99.9999991\%$ the speed of light at the Large Hadron Collider (CERN) and the Relativistic Heavy-Ion Collider (RHIC, BNL), we collide them to recreate temperatures exceeding $2\times 10^{12}\text{ K}$ (more than $100,000$ times hotter than the center of the Sun), liberating quarks and gluons into a laboratory-scale "Little Bang".

2. Anatomy of a Little Bang: Evolution of a Heavy-Ion Collision

The Quark-Gluon Plasma is extremely fleeting: it exists for only about $10^{-23}$ seconds inside a microscopic volume of a few tens of femtometers ($1\text{ fm} = 10^{-15}\text{ m}$). Because it cannot be photographed or observed directly, physicists reconstruct its properties by tracing its complete chronological evolution:

  1. Initial State & Pre-Equilibrium ($t < 1\text{ fm}/c$): Two Lorentz-contracted nuclei collide, depositing immense energy density. High-energy partons undergo initial hard scatterings, while fluctuating nucleon positions establish the initial spatial geometry.
  2. Quark-Gluon Plasma & Relativistic Hydrodynamics ($1 \lesssim t \lesssim 10\text{ fm}/c$): The dense medium thermalises and expands rapidly under intense internal pressure gradients. Remarkably, the QGP expands not as a gas, but as an almost perfect fluid with an extremely small ratio of shear viscosity to entropy density ($\eta/s \approx 1/4\pi$), near the quantum lower bound.
  3. Hadronization & Chemical Freeze-out ($T \approx 155\text{ MeV}$): As the fireball expands and cools, quarks and gluons recombine into hadrons. Inelastic collisions cease at the chemical freeze-out temperature ($T_{\mathrm{ch}}$), fixing the relative abundances of all particle species.
  4. Kinetic Freeze-out & Detection ($T \approx 90\text{--}120\text{ MeV}$): Elastic collisions stop, fixing the momentum distributions of all emitted particles. The stable particles and their decay products then travel outward into massive detectors such as ALICE.

3. Diagnosing the Fireball: Key Experimental Signatures

To diagnose whether a Quark-Gluon Plasma formed and to measure its physical properties, my research focuses on two paramount experimental signatures:

A. Anisotropic Flow ($v_n$): Transforming Geometry into Momentum

When two spherical nuclei collide with a non-zero impact parameter (non-central collision), their spatial overlap region is not circular, but rather almond-shaped (anisotropic). This spatial anisotropy causes asymmetric pressure gradients: the pressure gradient is steepest along the shortest axis of the almond.

As the fluid expands, these pressure gradients push particles outward faster along the reaction plane than perpendicular to it, translating initial spatial coordinate eccentricity ($\varepsilon_n$) into a final azimuthal momentum anisotropy of produced particles ($v_n$).

Mathematically, the azimuthal distribution of produced particles is decomposed into a Fourier series: $$ \frac{dN}{d\varphi} \propto 1 + 2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} v_n \cos\left[n(\varphi - \Psi_n)\right] $$ where $\varphi$ is the azimuthal emission angle, $\Psi_n$ is the $n$-th harmonic symmetry plane, and:

  • $v_2$ (Elliptic Flow): Probes the overall almond-shaped elliptic eccentricity ($\varepsilon_2$) and the equation of state of the medium.
  • $v_3$ (Triangular Flow): Originates from event-by-event quantum fluctuations in the initial nucleon positions ($\varepsilon_3$).
  • Higher Harmonics ($v_4, v_5$): Sensitive to viscous damping and non-linear mode-coupling effects in relativistic hydrodynamics.

B. Strangeness Enhancement: A Thermometer of Deconfinement

Ordinary matter is made entirely of first-generation quarks: up ($u$) and down ($d$). Strange quarks ($s$), being heavier ($m_s \sim 95\text{ MeV}/c^2$), are suppressed in ordinary elementary interactions due to energy thresholds.

However, inside a hot, deconfined QGP, abundant high-energy gluons interact via thermal fusion ($gg \to s\bar{s}$), rapidly producing strange and anti-strange quark pairs until chemical equilibrium is reached. Consequently, the yields of strange and multi-strange hadrons—such as $K^0_{\mathrm{S}}(d\bar{s})$, $\Lambda(uds)$, $\Xi^-(dss)$, and $\Omega^-(sss)$—normalized to non-strange pions ($\pi$), are substantially enhanced in heavy-ion collisions compared to baseline proton-proton collisions.

4. The "Small Systems" Puzzle at the LHC

Historically, the high-energy physics community believed that QGP could only form in violent collisions of large nuclei (such as Lead or Gold), whereas proton-proton ($pp$) collisions were treated as simple baseline vacuum systems.

However, experiments at the LHC uncovered a startling discovery: high-multiplicity proton-proton collisions exhibit unmistakable heavy-ion-like collective signatures! These include:

  • Non-zero elliptic flow ($v_2$) and ridge-like long-range two-particle angular correlations.
  • Characteristic mass-dependent push in average transverse momentum ($\langle p_{\mathrm{T}} \rangle$), indicative of radial hydrodynamic flow.
  • A smooth, continuous increase in strange and multi-strange hadron-to-pion ratios from $pp$ to $p\text{--Pb}$ and $\text{Pb}\text{--Pb}$ collisions.

Yet other classic heavy-ion signatures, such as jet quenching and heavy-quarkonium suppression, remain undetected in small systems. This raises one of the most compelling active questions in high-energy nuclear physics: Are we witnessing the creation of the smallest droplets of Quark-Gluon Plasma in proton collisions, or are these phenomena driven by microscopic multi-parton interactions (MPI), color reconnection, and rope hadronization?

5. Event-Shape Engineering: Piercing Multiplicity Bias

In collider experiments, collisions are traditionally categorized by charged-particle multiplicity ($N_{\mathrm{ch}}$). However, our investigations demonstrated that multiplicity selections suffer from severe selection bias: high multiplicity can be triggered either by a genuine, high-density soft thermal fireball OR by violent, multiple hard-scattering sprays (multi-jet topologies).

To untangle these physics mechanisms, my research develops and utilizes Event-Shape Engineering (ESE) with topological event classifiers:

Transverse Spherocity ($S_0$)

Classifies events based on the transverse momentum distribution with respect to a unit vector $\hat{n}$: $$ S_0 = \frac{\pi^2}{4} \min_{\hat{n}} \left( \frac{\sum_i |\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T},i} \times \hat{n}|}{\sum_i p_{\mathrm{T},i}} \right)^2 $$ Events with $S_0 \to 0$ are jetty (dominated by hard, back-to-back QCD dijets), while events with $S_0 \to 1$ are isotropic (azimuthally uniform, high-entropy, thermal-like emissions).

Charged-Particle Flattenicity ($\rho_{\mathrm{ch}}$)

A novel grid-based classifier dividing $(\eta, \varphi)$ phase space into equal cells. By measuring the variance of cell multiplicities, flattenicity isolates events governed by genuine Multi-Parton Interactions (MPI) while remaining free from auto-correlation biases and jet contamination.

6. Experimental Analysis with ALICE at CERN LHC Run 3

Beyond phenomenological modeling, my work directly engages with experimental data collected by the ALICE (A Large Ion Collider Experiment) detector during LHC Run 3 at a record center-of-mass energy of $\sqrt{s} = 13.6\text{ TeV}$.

My experimental focus includes:

  • Neutral Strange Particle Reconstruction ($K^0_{\mathrm{S}}$ and $\Lambda+\bar{\Lambda}$): Exploiting characteristic $V^0$ decay topologies ($K^0_{\mathrm{S}} \to \pi^+ \pi^-$ and $\Lambda \to p \pi^-$) reconstructed via secondary vertex finding.
  • Flattenicity-Dependent Production in Run 3: Measuring transverse momentum ($p_{\mathrm{T}}$) spectra and yield ratios as a function of $(1-\rho_{\mathrm{ch}})$ to isolate soft particle production mechanisms from hard QCD backgrounds.
  • Detector Tracking & PID: Utilizing the upgraded Monolithic Active Pixel Sensor-based Inner Tracking System (ITS2) for precision vertexing and the continuous-readout Time Projection Chamber (TPC) for ionization energy-loss ($dE/dx$) particle identification.

7. Frontiers: Nuclear Clustering & Artificial Intelligence

A. Exotic $\alpha$-Clustering in Light Nuclei ($^{16}\text{O}\text{--}^{16}\text{O}$)

Do light nuclei behave as uniform liquid drops of nucleons, or do they organize into geometric sub-clusters of $\alpha$-particles ($^4\text{He}$)? In collaboration with theorists and experimentalists, we probe the tetrahedral $\alpha$-clustered nuclear structure of Oxygen-16 by measuring harmonic flow fluctuations ($v_2, v_3, v_4$) in $^{16}\text{O}\text{--}^{16}\text{O}$ collisions at LHC energies within hydrodynamic and transport frameworks (CGC+Hydro, AMPT).

B. Machine Learning for Collider Physics

To address the computational complexity of modern collider data, I design deep learning models (Convolutional Neural Networks, Deep Multilayer Perceptrons, and Gradient Boosted Decision Trees):

  • Impact Parameter ($b$) Estimation: Reconstructing collision centrality and impact parameter event-by-event directly from final-state detector hit profiles.
  • Heavy-Flavor Identification: Classifying prompt vs. non-prompt $J/\psi$ and bottom-hadron decay kinematics in proton-proton collisions.
  • Deep Learning Flow Estimators: Formulating non-linear neural estimators to extract elliptic flow $v_2$ across low-multiplicity environments where conventional cumulant techniques degrade.
